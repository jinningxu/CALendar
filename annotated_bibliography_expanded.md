# Annotated Bibliography: California Natural Calendar (CALendar)

**Project:** CALendar — A bilingual (Chinese/English) month-view calendar web app showcasing 47 California native species, created for the IB11 California Natural History capstone project.

---

## Source 1

**Young, Allison M., Elizabeth F. van Mantgem, Alexis Garretson, Christine Noel, and Toni Lyn Morelli.** "Translational Science Education Through Citizen Science." *Frontiers in Environmental Science*, vol. 9, 2021, article 800433. doi:10.3389/fenvs.2021.800433.

K–12 students in Humboldt County used iNaturalist to run BioBlitz events, generating 5,000+ observations of 529 species. My calendar borrows this idea — putting a species on a specific day each month works like a mini BioBlitz, making discovery feel like a game instead of a list. Weakness: the study didn't formally measure what students learned.

---

## Source 2

**Rosemartin, Alyssa, Tim Watkins, and Abraham J. Miller-Rushing.** "Monitoring Phenology in US National Parks through Citizen Science: Some Preliminary Lessons and Prospects for Protected Areas." *Parks Stewardship Forum*, vol. 37, no. 3, 2021, pp. 552–60. doi:10.5070/P537354739.

This article describes how 36 U.S. national parks, including several in California, used the Nature's Notebook app to track seasonal events like flowering and bird migration. It backs up my calendar's two main design choices: organizing species by month (because phenology is about timing, not categories) and including Chinese holidays alongside Western ones (because different cultures track seasons differently). Weakness: it's about organized park programs, not self-guided apps like mine.

---

## Source 3

**Whitaker, Melissa R.L., Joshua Jiron, and Brian Maass.** "Developing Mobile Tools for Biodiversity Informatics and Natural History Education." *Journal of Natural History Education and Experience*, vol. 8, 2014, pp. 29–33.

This article presents *The Butterfly Guide*, a free iPhone app covering 117 butterfly species in the Sacramento Valley and Bay Area, with a built-in observation journal. It's the closest precedent for my calendar — a California-specific digital natural history tool for the public — and its journal feature directly inspired the Nature Journal textarea in my species popups. Weakness: from 2014, native iOS only; my calendar is a responsive website that works on any device.

---

## Source 4

**Holden, Meg, and Annika Airas.** "Translanguaging for More-than-English Sustainability Transitions." *Local Environment*, vol. 30, no. 5, 2025, pp. 691–708. doi:10.1080/13549839.2024.2436021.

This paper argues that 96.6% of sustainability research is published in English, shutting out non-English-speaking communities from environmental knowledge. It's the scholarly reason my calendar is fully bilingual — every species name, description, and holiday is in both Chinese and English, switchable with one click. Weakness: the paper focuses on Canadian/European languages, not Chinese-English bilingualism.

---

## Source 5

**Lacey, Eileen A., Talisin T. Hammond, Ryan E. Walsh, Kayce C. Bell, Scott V. Edwards, Elizabeth R. Ellwood, Robert Guralnick, et al.** "Climate Change, Collections and the Classroom: Using Big Data to Tackle Big Problems." *Evolution: Education and Outreach*, vol. 10, no. 1, 2017, article 2. doi:10.1186/s12052-017-0065-3.

College students analyzed real Yosemite animal surveys from 1914–1920 and 2003–2006, discovering that the alpine chipmunk shifted uphill by 500 meters due to climate change. Students were more engaged with real data than fake examples. This supports my choice to include real info for every species — scientific names, taxonomy, habitat ranges, conservation status, and iNaturalist photos — instead of generic nature facts. Weakness: targets biology students, not a general audience like my calendar.
