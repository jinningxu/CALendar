# Annotated Bibliography: California Natural Calendar (CALendar)

**Project:** CALendar — A bilingual (Chinese/English) month-view calendar web app showcasing California's endemic flora and fauna, designed for the IB11 California Natural History capstone project.

---

## Source 1

**Young, Allison M., Elizabeth F. van Mantgem, Alexis Garretson, Christine Noel, and Toni Lyn Morelli.** "Translational Science Education Through Citizen Science." *Frontiers in Environmental Science*, vol. 9, 2021, article 800433. doi:10.3389/fenvs.2021.800433.

This peer-reviewed article presents a case study of how the iNaturalist citizen science platform was used to conduct BioBlitzes across 19 sites in Humboldt County, California's coastal redwood ecosystem, engaging students from grade school through college alongside teachers and the Sequoia Park Zoo. The authors introduce Translational Science Education (TSE) as a framework adapted from Translational Ecology, demonstrating how citizen science projects can simultaneously generate scientifically useful biodiversity data while achieving meaningful educational outcomes. The study found that organized BioBlitzes significantly increased local biodiversity records and revealed an urbanization gradient with fewer non-native species at rural sites. A key strength of this source is its direct applicability: it provides a concrete model for how a digital biodiversity observation platform (iNaturalist) can be integrated into formal and informal education in California. One limitation is that the study focuses on structured, event-based BioBlitzes rather than the kind of sustained, self-directed engagement that a calendar app might encourage, so the findings on long-term user motivation are somewhat limited. Additionally, the study was conducted in a single region (Humboldt County), which may not fully generalize to the statewide scope of my calendar project. I intend to draw on this source to justify the educational value of connecting users to California species through a digital interface, and to support the idea that making biodiversity information accessible and location-specific — as my calendar does by associating species with months and seasons — can foster ecological literacy. The TSE framework also provides a conceptual vocabulary for describing how my project bridges scientific knowledge and public engagement.

**Relevance to CALendar:** Directly demonstrates how digital platforms (iNaturalist) can teach California biodiversity through seasonal, place-based observation — the same educational philosophy behind associating each month with specific species in my calendar.

## Source 2

**Rosemartin, Alyssa, Tim Watkins, and Abraham J. Miller-Rushing.** "Monitoring Phenology in US National Parks through Citizen Science: Some Preliminary Lessons and Prospects for Protected Areas." *Parks Stewardship Forum*, vol. 37, no. 3, 2021, pp. 552–63. doi:10.5070/P537354739.

This peer-reviewed article examines how the USA National Phenology Network's Nature's Notebook program has been implemented across 36 U.S. national parks to engage volunteers in tracking seasonal biological events — such as flowering, leaf-out, bird migration, and insect emergence. The authors report that over 500 organizations and thousands of volunteers have contributed more than five million phenological records, and they document how park staff have used these data for resource management, restoration planning, invasive species control, and climate change communication. A notable strength of this article is its dual focus: it provides evidence that phenology monitoring achieves both scientific data generation and public education goals, which directly parallels the dual purpose of my calendar (aesthetic appreciation of nature + factual natural history learning). The article's discussion of how seasonal observation helps visitors understand place-based climate change impacts is especially relevant. A weakness is that the article focuses primarily on institutional programs within national parks rather than on self-directed, individual-level tools like a web calendar; the user experience design considerations that are central to my project are not addressed. Additionally, the article does not discuss bilingual or multicultural approaches to engaging diverse audiences. I intend to use this source to ground my project in the established scientific practice of phenology — the study of seasonal natural events — and to argue that a calendar is a conceptually natural interface for presenting phenological information. The article's documentation of how seasonal awareness deepens people's connection to local ecosystems strongly supports the core design premise of my calendar.

**Relevance to CALendar:** Provides the scientific foundation for why a seasonal calendar is an ideal format for presenting biodiversity information — phenology is fundamentally about what happens in nature at different times of the year.

## Source 3

**Whitaker, Melissa R.L., Joshua Jiron, and Brian Maass.** "Developing Mobile Tools for Biodiversity Informatics and Natural History Education." *Journal of Natural History Education and Experience*, vol. 8, 2014, pp. 29–33.

This article describes the development of *The Butterfly Guide: Butterflies of the Sacramento Valley, Delta, and San Francisco Bay Area*, a free iOS mobile application that provides species pages for 117 California butterflies, a journal feature for recording personal observations, and educational resources. The authors drew content primarily from Dr. Art Shapiro's long-term butterfly monitoring site at UC Davis and integrated the app with the Butterflies and Moths of North America database. A major strength of this source is its practical demonstration of how digital tools can make regional natural history knowledge accessible to the general public — exactly what my calendar aims to do across multiple taxa rather than a single insect order. The authors also make their source code and documentation freely available, establishing a collaborative template-based approach to regional natural history guide development that informed my thinking about how my calendar could be adapted for other regions. A limitation is that the article was published in 2014, and the technological landscape for mobile and web applications has changed considerably since then; specific implementation details are dated. The article also does not rigorously evaluate user learning outcomes, so its claims about educational impact are more anecdotal than empirical. I intend to use this source as a precedent for California-specific digital natural history tools and to cite its argument that well-designed digital tools can enhance rather than detract from direct engagement with nature — a concern that is relevant to my project given that a screen-based calendar could be seen as mediating rather than encouraging outdoor experience.

**Relevance to CALendar:** Serves as a direct precedent for a California-focused digital natural history guide, validating the concept of using a regionally specific digital tool to promote biodiversity awareness.

## Source 4

**Holden, Meg, and Annika Airas.** "Translanguaging for More-than-English Sustainability Transitions." *Local Environment*, vol. 30, no. 1, 2025, pp. 41–58. doi:10.1080/13549839.2024.2436021.

This peer-reviewed article argues that translanguaging — the fluid use of multiple languages to communicate and make meaning — holds "unrecognised value for inclusive sustainability action competence." The authors critique the dominance of English in sustainability science, noting that up to 96.6% of citations in the field are in English, and present workshop-based research demonstrating that students built deeper understanding of sustainability concepts when allowed to move between languages in culturally contextualized ways. A key strength of this source is its theoretical rigor in articulating why multilingual approaches are not merely about translation or accessibility, but about epistemological inclusion — allowing different cultural frameworks for understanding nature to coexist and enrich each other. This directly validates the bilingual (Chinese/English) design of my calendar, which is not simply a translation exercise but an attempt to make California natural history accessible to Chinese-speaking audiences who may encounter it through different cultural lenses. A weakness is that the article is primarily theoretical and workshop-based rather than providing quantitative evidence of improved learning outcomes from bilingual environmental tools. The article also focuses on European and Indigenous language contexts, with no specific discussion of Chinese-English bilingualism. I intend to use this source to provide a scholarly rationale for the bilingual architecture of my calendar and to argue that offering California natural history content in both English and Chinese expands the audience for biodiversity education and enriches the learning experience by allowing users to engage with the material in their preferred linguistic and cultural framework.

**Relevance to CALendar:** Provides the theoretical foundation for why the bilingual (Chinese/English) feature of my calendar is not just a convenience but a meaningful contribution to inclusive environmental education.

## Source 5

**Lacey, Eileen A., Talisin T. Hammond, Ryan E. Walsh, Kayce C. Bell, Scott V. Edwards, Elizabeth R. Ellwood, Robert Guralnick, et al.** "Climate Change, Collections and the Classroom: Using Big Data to Tackle Big Problems." *Evolution: Education and Outreach*, vol. 10, no. 1, 2017, article 2. doi:10.1186/s12052-017-0065-3.

This peer-reviewed article presents an online, open-access educational module that uses digitized natural history collections from the historic Grinnell surveys of Yosemite National Park, California (1914–1920) and modern resurveys (2003–2006) to teach undergraduate students about climate change impacts on species distributions. Students analyze nearly a century of specimen data from two chipmunk species to test hypotheses about differential responses to climate change using the "Move, Adapt, or Die" framework. A major strength of this source is its demonstration of how California's rich natural history collections — some of the most comprehensive in the world — can be transformed into compelling digital educational experiences. The module's use of real specimen data to tell a story about ecological change over time is conceptually similar to how my calendar uses monthly species profiles to tell a story about California's seasonal biodiversity. A limitation is that the module targets university undergraduates with some biology background, whereas my calendar is designed for a general audience with no assumed prior knowledge; the article does not address how to adapt natural history content for broader public audiences. Additionally, the module focuses narrowly on climate change response, while my calendar has a broader goal of fostering general natural history appreciation and seasonal awareness. I intend to use this source to connect my project to the tradition of using California natural history collections and data for public education, and to cite its finding that digital, specimen-based educational resources can significantly improve student engagement with ecological concepts — supporting the premise that my calendar's species profiles have genuine educational value beyond aesthetic appeal.

**Relevance to CALendar:** Demonstrates the educational power of connecting the public to California's natural history records through a digital interface, lending scholarly credibility to the species-data backbone of my calendar.
